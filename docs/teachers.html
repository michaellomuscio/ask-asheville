What this is

Ask Asheville is a working 3D model of Asheville and Buncombe County, North Carolina: every road and building on the real terrain, Census data for every neighborhood, and simulated traffic that moves through a whole day. Students type a question in their own words, and the page works out an answer from that data and shows the evidence on the map.

It runs in a web browser. There is nothing to install and no sign-in. It is not a chatbot and it does not search the internet: it reads a question for the places, measures and times it knows, does the arithmetic, and says how it knows. When the data can’t answer a question, it says so instead of guessing.

It fits social studies, geography and civics (how a place works, who lives where, how public services reach people), math and statistics (medians, rates, distributions, scatter plots, margin of error), and science and engineering practices (using models, arguing from evidence, designing under limits). We have not matched it to any particular set of standards; you are the best judge of where it fits your course.

How students use it

The ask bar

The bar at the top takes a question (“Where do the most kids live?”, “How long does it take to drive from Black Mountain to downtown?”) or the name of a place, school or address to fly to. Suggestions appear as students type.

Answer cards

Each answer appears on a card: a short headline, one or two sentences, the numbers behind it, often a chart, and a note on what the map is now showing. Every number carries one of four labels:

  • CountedAn actual count, like the 2020 Census.
  • SurveyedAn estimate from a sample survey. It has a margin of error.
  • SimulatedCalculated by the model of the city: drive times, traffic, response times.
  • MeasuredMeasured directly, like elevation or a traffic counter.

Under How do we know? each card lists where its numbers come from, what to keep in mind about them, and the vocabulary a student needs. “Ask next” offers follow-up questions.

Map layers and tools

The buttons on the left open the rest: Questions (the guided investigations below, more questions to try, and a glossary), Layers (color every neighborhood by one measure), Drive (drive-time maps), the siren button (emergency response: send a call, map response times, add a station), Day (switch to a snow day, a ballgame, leaf season and more) and Places. Clicking any building, road or vehicle shows what it is.

The notebook

Save to notebook on an answer card stores the answer, its numbers and sources, and a picture of the map as an evidence card. In the notebook students add their own notes, choose which cards to use as evidence, and write a short Claim–Evidence–Reasoning piece: their question, a claim, the evidence, their reasoning, and what they still wonder. They can print it, save it as a PDF, download it as a file or copy it as text to hand in.

A 45-minute first lesson

Works with students in pairs at one device, or with one projected screen and the class deciding together.

  1. 5 min
    Wonder

    Show the map on a projector. Ask: “What do you wonder about our city?” Write three or four questions on the board. Type one into the ask bar as the class watches.

  2. 5 min
    Read one answer together

    Read the card aloud: the headline, the numbers and their labels, the note about the map. Open “How do we know?” and ask: which of these numbers would you trust most, and why?

  3. 20 min
    Investigate in pairs

    Pairs open one investigation (links below; “Does where you live change how fast help arrives?” and “Why is five o’clock so slow?” are good first ones). They make a prediction before any data, work through the steps, and save at least two evidence cards.

  4. 10 min
    Write a claim

    In the notebook, each pair writes one claim that answers the question, marks the cards they are using as evidence, and writes two or three sentences of reasoning.

  5. 5 min
    Share and push back

    Two pairs read their claims. The class asks only two questions: “What is your evidence?” and “What can’t this data tell us?” Exit ticket: one new question you would ask next.

If time is short, print or download the notebooks at the bell. A notebook lives in that browser on that device, so it will not follow a student to another computer.

The investigations

Each one takes a single big question through four moves: predict before seeing any data, explore in four or five steps (each with a prompt to write what they notice, and often a “check your reading” item worked out live from the data), explain with a claim and evidence, and go further with new questions. Progress and notes are saved in the browser. The link under each one opens it directly, so you can post it for a class.

The list of investigations loads here. If it does not appear, open Ask Asheville and press Questions to see them.

Send students a question by link

Any question can be a link. Add #q= and the question to the address, and the page opens with that question already answered. The link button at the bottom of every answer card copies the link for you. Three examples:

  • Where do the most kids live?
  • How long does it take to drive from Black Mountain to downtown?
  • Is income related to college degrees?

An investigation opens the same way, with #i= and its short name, for example #i=help-arrives. Links only say which question to ask. They carry nothing about the student who opens them.

What the data is, and what it is not

Where it comes from

  • OpenStreetMap: roads, building outlines, water, places.
  • 2020 Census: people and homes, block by block.
  • American Community Survey, 2020–2024: age, income, housing, schooling and commuting for neighborhood areas, towns, the county, the state and the nation.
  • Census jobs data (LEHD), 2023: where jobs are and who commutes where.
  • Federal school lists: schools and enrollment.
  • USGS: elevation and land cover.
  • Buncombe County GIS: addresses and school attendance districts.
  • NCDOT: traffic counts at stations on main roads.
  • Local agency and news reports: fire, ambulance and police stations, hospitals, venues, visitor estimates.

What is modeled

  • People in homes. Residents are placed in buildings so every Census block adds up to its real 2020 count. The map reports people only for Census areas.
  • Traffic. A simulation sends trips over the real roads: commutes from Census work data, school runs, errands, deliveries and visitors. It imitates a typical day. It is not live traffic.
  • Drive times. Calculated from road speeds that were checked against an independent routing service, then slowed by the simulated traffic.
  • Emergency response. Calculated from station locations and road speeds, plus time to take the call and get rolling. It assumes a crew is in the station and ready.
  • Events and other days. What-ifs. Some crowd and visitor sizes are estimates.

Limits worth saying out loud

  • Survey numbers are estimates. For a small area or a small town the margin of error can be large. Treat close results as a tie. For a town’s median values, the answer card gives the margin of error.
  • Neighborhoods have no official edges. “West Asheville” on the map is the Census areas around a label, a close guess and not a boundary. Towns use their true Census boundaries.
  • Numbers have dates. The count is from 2020, the survey covers 2020 to 2024, school enrollment is a year or two old. Hurricane Helene in September 2024 changed things the older numbers cannot show.
  • Areas are not people. A number for a neighborhood says nothing about any one household in it.
  • Much is simply not here. There is no data on crime, flooding or storm damage, bus routes, sidewalks and bike lanes, parks, restaurants and stores, school test scores, or voting. On race and ethnicity it has only the share of residents who are Black and the share who are Hispanic or Latino. Asked about these, the page says it has no data.
  • Building heights and names come from volunteers and are incomplete. Ambulance base locations are approximate.

Privacy

  • No accounts and no tracking. There is no sign-in, and the page has no analytics or advertising.
  • Nothing a student types or saves leaves the device. Questions are answered in the browser from data that has already loaded. The notebook, investigation notes and “my spot” are kept in that browser’s own storage.
  • That storage is local. Clearing the browser’s site data, using a private window, or moving to another device loses the notebook. Have students print it, save it as a PDF or download it before they leave.
  • No student names are stored. The printed notebook has blank lines for a name and date to fill in by hand.
  • People are reported only for Census areas, never for a single home. Clicking a house shows its address (a public county record), the kind of building, and facts about the surrounding Census area. It never shows who lives there or what a household earns.
  • On first load the page fetches its map data, fonts and a 3D graphics library from the web, as any web page does. After that, questions need no connection.

What it needs

  • A recent version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari with WebGL 2 (nearly every laptop, Chromebook and tablet from the last several years).
  • About 22 MB to load the first time. On a slow connection, have students open it at the start of class.
  • It works on Chromebooks. On a modest machine it lowers the graphics detail and simulates fewer vehicles on its own to stay smooth; the settings button at the bottom has the same controls.
  • On a very slow machine, add ?nosim to the address (for example ./?nosim) to get the map, the layers and most questions without moving traffic. Questions about different days and events need the traffic running.
  • A phone will run it, but the panels are built for a laptop-sized screen.

Talking about sensitive topics

The map shows income, poverty, rent, home values, race and ethnicity for small areas. Students will look up places they know, including their own. A few habits help:

  • Set norms first. The data describes areas, not people and not classmates. No one has to say where they live, and no one guesses about anyone else.
  • Say “this area” and not “those people”. A median or a share describes a place with hundreds or thousands of residents who differ from one another.
  • Ask why before judging. Patterns in housing and income have histories: decisions about roads, zoning, lending and investment made over many decades. The map shows the pattern, not the cause. Pair it with local history and with people who can speak to it.
  • Keep the limits in view. Small-area estimates are uncertain, and a pattern between two measures does not prove that one causes the other. The investigation “Do these two things go together?” is built around this.
  • Let students ask what is missing. “What can’t this data tell us?” is part of every investigation and is often the best discussion of the day.
